# Emberly Oxygen 0.1.1 — Release note

Nov 7, 2026 · @Wattanit

**One technique: partial sums in the matrix-vector product.** 0.1.1 changes one thing, the inner loop of the matrix-vector product, and measures it against the [0.1.0](0.1.0.md) rows. Generation is 6.2× faster on the short workload and 4.3× on the long one; the kernel itself is 6.4× to 8.3× faster. Built against Requirements 0.2 and Technical Specification 0.2 (the Specification's status is still `draft`; the owner sets it). The detailed record is the [implementation plan](../emberly-oxygen-implementation-plan-0.1.1.md) (§4 measurements, §5 feedback for the next foundation bump) and the per-phase to-dos in [`docs/todo/`](../todo/).

## What changed

- **The dot product keeps 16 independent partial sums instead of one running total** (FR-25, Spec §4). Sum *j* adds the products at indices ≡ *j* mod 16, each in index order; the 16 sums are then added to each other from lane 0, and the last `len mod 16` products are added in index order after that. The result is the same on every run, bit for bit (S-6), and differs from 0.1.0's in the last bits, because float addition is not associative.
- **Still safe, single-threaded Rust.** No `unsafe` in the kernels, no SIMD intrinsics, no threads, no tiling: those are deferred (Requirements §2.2), so this release measures one technique alone. Every other operation still adds in index order. (0.1.0's note and README said the engine had no `unsafe` at all; that was imprecise. The loader has one, the `Mmap::map` call for the model file, with a safety comment, as it did in 0.1.0.)
- **The width, 16, came from a sweep, not from the Specification's initial 8.** The rule was fixed before measuring: the width with the lowest estimated kernel time per token (calls per token × median ns per call, summed over the five shapes), ties within 2% going to the smaller. Two dry runs of widths 4, 8, 16 and 32 both chose 16: 7.47 and 7.42 ms per token, against 9.27 and 9.30 for 8 sums, 9.20 and 9.02 for 32, 15.3 and 15.5 for 4, and 48.8 and 49.3 for the 0.1.0 loop.
- **The benchmark's kernel group times seven variants** at the model's five shapes: the engine, `matrixmultiply`, the 0.1.0 loop (kept as a reference) and widths 4, 8, 16 and 32. The four 0.1.0 field names are unchanged, so old and new rows compare.
- **The test suite gained** kernel tests (agreement with two references at the model's shapes and at every length from 0 to 40, exactness, reproducibility, two order pins and three panic checks); **214 tests** now run in about 28 seconds.

## Why it was expected to help

A dot product written as `sum += w * x` is a chain: each addition waits for the one before, so the processor's adders sit idle for most of their latency. Several independent sums break the chain, so the additions overlap, and the compiler can then turn the loop into vector instructions. Phase 4 of 0.1.0 had measured this: 4 sums about 2×, 8 sums about 5×.

Against `matrixmultiply` the engine is now about 5× faster at the four small shapes and 7.9× at the output projection. Model load is unchanged at about 60 ms. The whole bench takes about 4 minutes (233 s; 0.1.0: 306 s).

**What the speed-up is worth for a step.** Step time fell from 52.7 to 8.5 ms (short) and from 54.1 to 12.5 ms (long). The matrix-vector product, estimated from the kernel rows (60, 60, 60, 30 and 1 calls per token), fell from 49.7 to 7.3 ms per token, so its share of a step fell from 94% to 86% on the short workload and to 58% on the long one. 0.1.0's note said 98.6%; that came from a dry run on a cooler machine, and 94% is the like-for-like figure from the recorded rows. The shares are estimates (the product is timed alone, on a hot cache). The result: on the long workload the work outside the product, mostly attention over 512 to 640 cached positions, is about 5.2 ms, 42% of a step. That is why long generation gained 4.3× where the kernel gained 6.4× to 8.3×; this is a hypothesis from the numbers, not a profile.

## Correctness

The oracle comparison passes on every prompt in every category, with thresholds unchanged (S-1 1e-3 absolute, S-2 1e-4 relative). Worst measured, 16 sums against 0.1.0's:

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| english | 3.14e-4 | 1.81e-4 | 3.66e-5 | 4.78e-6 |
| thai | 2.79e-4 | 2.36e-4 | 1.51e-5 | 5.38e-6 |
| code | 3.18e-4 | 1.97e-4 | 4.02e-5 | 5.91e-6 |
| utf8-split | **9.90e-4** | 2.16e-4 | 2.70e-5 | 4.59e-6 |

Top-token and greedy-continuation checks pass, logging changes nothing (S-8), and runs are bit-identical on one load and on a second (S-6).

**The margin on `utf8-split-cjk` is 99% of S-1** (22% in 0.1.0, 96% with 8 sums). It passes deterministically on these fixtures, but a correct engine is one rounding away from failing, and the owner kept S-1 for this release knowing it.

## What was learned

- **The harness errors moved against the reference, and the engine did not get less accurate.** Any partial-sum order moved the logits 1.4× to 8× further from the reference (widths 4 to 32 all about the same; fused multiply-add did not remove the move: with 1 sum it changed nothing, with 8 sums the error stayed 9.78e-4). A float64 forward pass of the same weights, run once as a diagnostic (its script and dumps are not in the repository), gave each engine's distance to the truth: the f32 reference itself is up to 7.1e-4 away (`utf8-split-cjk`), the 0.1.0 loop 5.1e-4, the 8-sum kernel 3.3e-4. The 8-sum kernel was closer to the truth than the 0.1.0 loop on 11 of 12 prompts. The 0.1.0 loop looked close because it sums in nearly the order PyTorch does, so their errors are correlated. The 16-sum kernel was not run through the float64 diagnostic. **S-1 measures distance to PyTorch's rounding, not to the correct answer;** a more accurate kernel can look worse to it.
- **Two remedies are recorded for the next Requirements bump** (plan §5, owner's choice): add a float64 reference to the oracle, so S-1 can judge against the truth; or loosen S-1 (for example to 2e-3, with the 2e-3 negative control raised to match). The trigger to act is a correct engine failing S-1 on fixtures regenerated on another machine, which is now plausible. S-2 has room (worst 40% of its limit).
- **The loop vectorizes in safe code.** The release assembly (aarch64) loads the weights and inputs and does `fmul.4s` and `fadd.4s` into 4-lane accumulators, not fused. With 8 sums that is two vector accumulators (the assembly was inspected at 8 sums only); 16 sums should give four, which would hide more add latency and fits its 20% gain over 8, though that reading is a hypothesis. 32 sums needs eight accumulators plus loads and loses to 16 everywhere (register pressure is the likely reason; no one inspected the spills).
- **The kernel reaches about 37 GFLOP/s,** against 5.8 for one sum. Taking only the safe single-threaded route, that is the step this release delivers; intrinsics, tiling and threads remain.
- **The test suite is 3× faster** (86 s to 28 s), mostly because the harness's 12 prompts run the same forward passes with the faster kernel.
- **The tokenizer's 21 dropped bytes** (0.1.0's finding) are now recorded in Requirements 0.2 (a note under FR-13), which also clarifies S-2's tensor definition.

## Negative results

- **A combining-order pin cannot fail against the 0.1.0 loop.** The plan said both order pins would fail against it; only the structure pin does. Corrected in the plan during Phase 7; the combining-order pin was shown able to fail with a reversed and a tree order.
- **`matrixmultiply` is still a weak yardstick,** now far behind (5× to 8×) and no use for judging how fast this kernel could be.
- **32 sums did not help,** and 4 sums gives only half the gain. The sweep otherwise found nothing unexpected.
- **Fusing multiply-add was no remedy** in the diagnostic runs: with 8 sums the worst logit error was 9.78e-4 against 9.59e-4 without it.

## What comes next

The matrix-vector product is no longer nearly all of a step; on long prompts about 42% is elsewhere, so profile attention and the other operations before further kernel work. Roadmap v3 (batched prompts) and further kernel techniques (intrinsics, tiling, threads) stay on the table; v2 (sampling and chat) is next in the roadmap. The next Requirements bump absorbs the S-1 decision above, and the Specification bump names width 16 in §4 and the sweep in §12 (plan §5).
